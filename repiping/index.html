Whole-House Repiping in Fort Walton Beach, FL

Repiping replaces the water supply pipes in a house when old material is leaking, corroding or restricting flow. It is a planned project, and a good time to do it is after a second or third leak, before water damage piles up.

When repiping makes sense

One leak does not mean a house needs new pipes. A pattern does. If you have repaired two or three leaks in a few years, if water is rusty or low in pressure at several fixtures or if the pipe is a material known for failing, repiping starts to cost less than repeated repairs.

A plumber can look at the pipe, test the pressure and talk through whether a full repipe, a partial one or targeted repairs is the better path. The answer depends on the pipe material, the age and where the problems are.

Old pipe types and what they signal

The material in the walls tells a lot about what is coming.

Pipe materialTypical problemUsual advice
Galvanized steelInternal rust, low pressure, discolored waterReplace, since failure is progressive
CopperPinholes, corrosion at jointsRepair if localized, repipe if widespread
Gray polybutyleneBrittle failure at fittingsReplace the system
CPVCBecomes brittle with ageReplace sections that fail
PEXFitting leaks, sun damageRepair, rarely needs replacing

If you are not sure what your pipes are made of, a plumber can tell by looking at the pipe where it enters the house or runs through the crawlspace or attic.

PEX or copper

PEX is flexible plastic tubing that is quick to install and resists corrosion and freeze damage. It runs in long lengths with fewer joints, and it can often be fished through walls with less wall damage. Copper is rigid metal with a long track record and a higher material cost.

Either can be a good choice. PEX is common in newer repiping jobs because of speed and cost, and copper appeals to homeowners who prefer metal. Water conditions can affect copper over time, so a water test and a look at your utility report help you decide.

How a repiping project runs

A repipe follows a clear sequence, and a good crew limits the disruption.

1

Inspect and plan

The plumber maps the pipe runs, fixtures and access points.

2

Shut off and set up

Water is shut off and protection is laid down in the work area.

3

Run new lines

New supply lines are installed through attic, crawlspace or wall openings.

4

Connect and test

Fixtures are connected and the system is pressure tested.

5

Patch access holes

Openings in walls are closed, and finish repairs are discussed.

Ask how long the water will be off each day. Most projects restore service at the end of each work day.

Cast iron drains and older homes

Repiping the supply side does not change the drain lines. Older Fort Walton Beach homes may also have cast iron drain pipes that rust from the inside and crack with age. Repairs on cast iron are usually done by cutting out the damaged section and replacing it with PVC or a no-hub coupling.

If you are planning a major remodel, it is smart to inspect the drains with a camera at the same time. Our sewer inspection page explains how that works. For pipes that have already failed, burst pipe repair covers the repair side.

Slab homes and repiping

Many local homes sit on concrete slabs, and supply lines may run beneath the floor. If a line under a slab keeps leaking, the common alternative to breaking the floor is a reroute through the attic or walls. This is a form of repiping that can save floors and last longer than spot repairs. Learn more on our slab leak repair page.

Repiping, permits and inspections

A repipe is permitted work in most places. New supply piping has to meet the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and the local building department may inspect it.

When to call us: after a pinhole leak, discolored water or a run of repairs, and before you open any walls.

Repiping questions

Many homes take a few days, depending on size and access. The plumber gives you a schedule after inspecting the house.

Some access holes are needed. PEX can often be routed with smaller openings than rigid pipe, and the plumber can show you where openings will go.

If leaks are isolated, repair. If the pipe is failing everywhere, repiping usually costs less in the long run.
